Reproductive Health
The term simply refers to healthy reproductive organs with normal functions, but it has a broader perspective
and includes the emotional and social aspects of reproduction also.
According to the WHO, reproductive health means a total well-being in all aspects of reproduction, i.e.,
physical, emotional, behavioural and social. Therefore, a society with people having physically and
functionally normal reproductive organs and normal emotional and behavioural interactions among them
in all sex-related aspects might be called reproductively healthy.
3.1
REPRODUCTIVE HEALTH -- PROBLEMS AND STRATEGIES
India moved early on reproductive health -- timeline as NCERT states:
• Programme launch -- 1951: India was amongst the first countries in the world (Summary: the first nation)
to initiate action plans and programmes at national level to attain total reproductive health as a social goal --
these programmes, called family planning, were initiated in 1951 and periodically assessed over past
decades.
• Current name: improved programmes covering wider reproduction-related areas now run as Reproductive
and Child Health Care (RCH) programmes.
• Major tasks under RCH: creating awareness about reproduction-related aspects and providing facilities and
support for a reproductively healthy society.
3.1
Creating awareness
Under RCH, creating awareness is a major task. Different actors play distinct roles:
• Governmental and non-governmental agencies -- steps through audio-visual and print-media.
• Parents, close relatives, teachers, friends -- major role in dissemination of reproduction-related information.
• Schools -- sex education encouraged, gives right information and discourages myths and misconceptions about
sex.
What information is given depends on the audience:
• Adolescent age group especially -- reproductive organs, adolescence and related changes, safe and
hygienic sexual practices, STDs, AIDS -- to lead a reproductively healthy life.
• Fertile couples and those in marriageable age -- birth control options, care of pregnant mothers,
post-natal care of mother and child, importance of breast feeding, equal opportunities for male and
female child -- to bring up socially conscious healthy families of desired size.
• General society -- problems of uncontrolled population growth and social evils like sex-abuse and
sex-related crimes -- to enable people to prevent them and build a socially responsible and healthy society.
3.1
Infrastructure, programmes and research
Execution needs more than awareness -- it needs systems and science on the ground:
• Strong infrastructural facilities, professional expertise and material support -- to provide medical
assistance and care for pregnancy, delivery, STDs, abortions, contraception, menstrual problems,
infertility.
• Better techniques and new strategies from time to time -- for more efficient care; research on
reproduction-related areas is encouraged and supported by governmental and non-governmental agencies --
to find out new methods and/or to improve upon the existing ones.
Two programmes NCERT singles out as examples of that infrastructure in action:
• Statutory ban on amniocentesis for sex-determination (source prints aminocentesis) -- legally checks the
increasing menace of female foeticides.
• Massive child immunisation -- merits mention in this connection.

Amniocentesis: some of the amniotic fluid of the developing foetus is taken to analyse the fetal cells and
dissolved substances. This procedure is used to test for the presence of certain genetic disorders such as down
syndrome, haemophilia, sickle-cell anemia, etc., and to determine the survivability of the foetus -- the source
sentence prints haemoplilia for haemophilia and is garbled, reproduced here with the correct term.
[NOTE] Saheli -- a new oral contraceptive for the females -- was developed by scientists at Central Drug
Research Institute (CDRI) in Lucknow, India.
That the system works is visible in outcome indicators:
• Better awareness about sex-related matters -- up.
• Medically assisted deliveries -- increased in number.
• Better post-natal care -- decreased MMR and IMR.
• Couples with small families -- increased in number.
• Better detection and cure of STDs -- better.
• Overall medical facilities for all sex-related problems -- increased.
• Assistance to infertile couples -- added in Summary as an extra indicator.

3.2
Surgical methods -- sterilisation
Surgical methods, also called sterilisation, are generally advised for the male/female partner as a terminal
method to prevent any more pregnancies. Surgical intervention blocks gamete transport and thereby
prevent conception. Sterilisation procedure in the male is called vasectomy and that in the female,
tubectomy.
1
Vasectomy: a small part of the vas deferens is removed or tied up through a small incision on the
scrotum (Figure 3.4a) -- Vas deferens tied and cut.
2
Tubectomy: a small part of the fallopian tube is removed (Figure 3.4b) or tied up through a small
incision in the abdomen or through vagina -- Fallopian tubes tied and cut.
These techniques are highly effective but their reversibility is very poor.

Putting contraception in perspective -- NCERT caveats at the end of 3.2:
• Who decides: selection and use should always be in consultation with qualified medical professionals.
• Nature: not regular requirements for maintenance of reproductive health; practiced against a natural
event -- conception/pregnancy; one is forced to use them either to prevent or to delay/space pregnancy due
to personal reasons.
• Population role: widespread use has significant role in checking uncontrolled growth of population.
• Caution -- possible ill-effects: nausea, abdominal pain, breakthrough bleeding, irregular menstrual
bleeding or even breast cancer -- though not very significant, should not be totally ignored.

3.3
MEDICAL TERMINATION OF PREGNANCY (MTP)
Intentional or voluntary termination of pregnancy before full term is called medical termination of
pregnancy (MTP) or induced abortion -- the opener defines the term used in its own section heading.
• Global scale: nearly 45 to 50 million MTPs per year worldwide = 1/5th of total conceived pregnancies;
whether to accept/legalise is debated in many countries for emotional, ethical, religious and social issues.
• Indian law: Government of India legalised MTP in 1971 with strict conditions to avoid misuse; restrictions
important to check indiscriminate and illegal female foeticides reported high in India.
3.3
Why MTP, and when it is safest
• Why MTP? To get rid of unwanted pregnancies -- due to casual unprotected intercourse, failure of
contraceptive used during coitus, or rapes; also essential where continuation could be harmful or even
fatal to mother or foetus or both.
• When is it safest? Relatively safe during first trimester -- upto 12 weeks; second trimester abortions are
much more risky.
• Majority of MTPs performed illegally by unqualified quacks -- not only unsafe but could be fatal.
• Misuse of amniocentesis to determine sex of unborn child -- frequently, if foetus found female, followed by
MTP -- totally against what is legal; dangerous for young mother and foetus.
• Reversal: effective counselling to avoid unprotected coitus and on risk factors of illegal abortions, plus
more health care facilities, could reverse these trends.
3.3
The MTP (Amendment) Act, 2017
[NOTE] The Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2017 was enacted by the government of India
with the intension of reducing the incidence of illegal abortion and consequent maternal mortality and morbidity
-- source prints intension for intention.
Under the Act, a pregnancy may be terminated on certain considered grounds and within strict time limits:
1
Within the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, on the opinion of one registered medical practitioner.
2
More than 12 weeks but fewer than 24 weeks, on the opinion of two registered medical practitioners,
formed in good faith, that the required grounds exist.
3
Ground (i): continuation of the pregnancy would involve a risk to the life of the pregnant woman or of
grave injury to her physical or mental health.
4
Ground (ii): there is a substantial risk that if the child were born, it would suffer from such physical or
mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped.
3.4
SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS (STIs)
Infections or diseases which are transmitted through sexual intercourse are collectively called sexually
transmitted infections (STI) or venereal diseases (VD) or reproductive tract infections (RTI) -- the opener
defines the term used in its own heading.
• Common examples (8 named): gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts,
trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B and HIV leading to AIDS (the most discussed infection in recent years) -- among
these HIV is most dangerous (Chapter 7).
• Other routes for hepatitis-B and HIV: also transmitted by sharing injection needles, surgical instruments
with infected persons, transfusion of blood, or from infected mother to foetus.
• Curability: all except hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV are completely curable if detected early and
treated properly.

3.4
Symptoms, stigma and complications
• Early symptoms of most of these (minor): itching, fluid discharge, slight pain, swellings, etc., in genital
region.
• Asymptomatic cases: infected females may often be asymptomatic and may remain undetected for long.
• Why timely treatment is deterred: absence or less significant early symptoms plus social stigma attached to
STIs deter infected persons from timely detection and proper treatment.
• Late complications if untreated: pelvic inflammatory diseases (PID), abortions, still births, ectopic
pregnancies, infertility or even cancer of reproductive tract -- STIs are a major threat to a healthy society;
prevention / early detection and cure given prime consideration under RCH.
• Most vulnerable age group: all persons vulnerable but incidences very high among 15-24 years -- the age
group to which you also belong.
3.4
Prevention is possible
There is no reason to panic because prevention is possible -- three simple principles keep one free of these
infections:
1
Avoid sex with unknown partners/multiple partners.
2
Always try to use condoms during coitus.
3
In case of doubt, go to a qualified doctor for early detection and get complete treatment if diagnosed
with infection.

3.5
Artificial insemination and the limits of ART
• AI -- artificial insemination / IUI -- intra-uterine insemination: for infertility due to inability of male
partner to inseminate the female or very low sperm counts in the ejaculates -- semen from husband or
healthy donor artificially introduced into vagina or uterus (IUI) of female.
Why ART does not reach everyone:
• Technical: extremely high precision handling by specialised professionals and expensive instrumentation
required.
• Access: presently available only in very few centres in the country; affordable to only a limited number of
people.
• Acceptance: emotional, religious and social factors are deterrents in adoption.
[NOTE] ZIFT vs IUT is a numbers test: ZIFT -- zygote or early embryo upto 8 blastomeres -- into the fallopian
tube; IUT -- embryos with more than 8 blastomeres -- into the uterus. GIFT transfers an unfertilised ovum, IVF
transfers an embryo, ICSI injects a single sperm into a single ovum, and IUI introduces semen into the uterus --
four different cargos, four different names.
3.5
Adoption as an alternative
Since the ultimate aim of all these procedures is to have children, in India we have so many orphaned and
destitute children, who would probably not survive till maturity, unless taken care of. Our laws permit legal
adoption and it is as yet, one of the best methods for couples looking for parenthood.

• Reproductive health is total well-being -- physical, emotional, behavioural and social (WHO). India was
amongst the first countries / the first nation to launch RCH programmes (family planning, 1951). The
work is awareness (audio-visual, print, parents/teachers, sex education) plus medical care for pregnancy,
delivery, STDs, contraception, menstrual problems and infertility, supported by infrastructure, expertise,
research and laws such as the ban on amniocentesis for sex determination. Indicators of improvement are
lower MMR/IMR, more small families, better STD cure, more medical facilities and assistance to infertile
couples -- plus Saheli from CDRI, Lucknow.
• Population: better health and living conditions drove an explosion -- world 2 billion (1900) to 6 billion (2000)
to 7.2 billion (2011); India about 350 million at independence to near a billion by 2000 to over 1.2 billion
in May 2011. Reasons: fall in death rate, MMR, IMR and more people in reproducible age. RCH cut
growth only marginally to less than 2% (20/1000/year, 2011), threatening food, shelter, clothing scarcity.
Response: motivate smaller families -- Hum Do Hamare Do, one-child norm, marriage age 18 (F) / 21 (M),
incentives.
• Contraception -- seven NCERT groups: an ideal contraceptive is user-friendly, easily available, effective,
reversible, least side effects and does not interfere with the sexual act. Natural -- avoid ovum-sperm
meeting without devices: periodic abstinence day 10-17 (fertile period), withdrawal, lactational
amenorrhea (intense lactation, 6 months only, almost nil side effects but high failure). Barrier -- physically
block meeting: condoms (Nirodh, also protects from STIs/AIDS, disposable, private),
diaphragms/caps/vaults (reusable) plus spermicidal creams/jellies/foams. IUDs by doctors / nurses through
vagina: Lippes loop, CuT/Cu7/Multiload 375, Progestasert/LNG-20 via phagocytosis, Cu suppression,
hormone making uterus unsuitable and cervix hostile; ideal for spacing, most widely accepted in India.
Pills -- progestogen or progestogen-estrogen 21 days within first 5 days, 7-day gap, inhibit
ovulation/implantation and alter cervical mucus; well accepted, plus Saheli weekly, non-steroidal.
Injectables/implants under skin, similar mode but much longer action. Emergency within 72 hours after
rape or casual unprotected intercourse. Surgical sterilisation blocks gamete transport -- vasectomy (Vas
deferens tied and cut) and tubectomy (Fallopian tubes tied and cut), highly effective, poorly reversible --
always chosen with a qualified doctor. Contraception is against natural conception, used only to prevent /
delay / space pregnancy, and possible ill-effects (nausea, bleeding, breast cancer) though not very
significant should not be ignored.
• MTP: intentional / voluntary termination before full term (induced abortion); 45-50 million per year =
1/5th of conceived pregnancies; debated for emotional, ethical, religious, social reasons; legal in India since

1971 with strict conditions to check illegal female foeticide; needed for unwanted pregnancies (casual /
failed contraception / rape) or harm to mother / foetus; relatively safe upto 12 weeks, riskier in second
trimester; majority illegal by quacks, unsafe / fatal; amniocentesis misused for sex determination leading
to female foeticide, dangerous for mother and foetus; reversible only by counselling, avoiding unprotected
coitus and more health facilities. Amendment Act, 2017 aims to cut illegal abortion / mortality: within 12
weeks -- one doctor; 12-24 weeks -- two doctors; grounds: risk to life / grave injury to physical / mental
health, or substantial risk of serious physical / mental handicap in the child.
• STIs: infections transmitted by sexual intercourse -- STI / VD / RTI: gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes,
chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B, HIV leading to AIDS (most dangerous, Chapter
7); hepatitis-B / HIV also spread by needles, surgical instruments, blood transfusion, mother to foetus; all
except hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV are completely curable if early and properly treated; minor
early symptoms -- itching, discharge, pain, swelling; females often asymptomatic; absent/less symptoms
plus stigma deter timely treatment leading to PID, abortions, still births, ectopic pregnancies, infertility,
reproductive-tract cancer; major threat, prevention / early cure is prime under RCH; highest incidence
15-24 years; prevention: avoid unknown / multiple partners, use condoms, see a qualified doctor if in
doubt and complete treatment.
• Infertility -- inability to produce children inspite of unprotected co-habitation even after 2 years; many
causes -- physical, congenital, diseases, drugs, immunological, psychological; female often blamed but
more often male partner is responsible; infertility clinics help diagnosis / corrective treatment, but where
not possible, ART assists: IVF-ET (test tube baby) -- ova and sperms collected, zygote formed in lab

factors; many orphaned / destitute children unlikely to survive without care -- legal adoption is as yet one
of the best methods for parenthood.

Q7 -- Why removal of gonads
is not a contraceptive option
The chapter's surgical methods are sterilisation by blocking gamete transport -- Vas deferens
tied and cut (vasectomy) and Fallopian tubes tied and cut (tubectomy); they do not remove
the gonads, and reversibility is already very poor. The chapter never lists gonad removal
among its seven contraceptive categories, and permanent removal violates the
ideal-contraceptive criteria of reversibility and no interference with normal physiology.
Hence gonad removal is not a contraceptive option.
Q11(a) -- Abortions can
happen spontaneously -- True
The chapter defines only intentional / voluntary termination (MTP / induced abortion), but it
also lists abortion as a complication of STIs / PID that occurs without any MTP procedure,
which shows pregnancy loss can happen on its own. Spontaneous abortion (miscarriage) is
therefore true.
Q12(c) -- Oral pills are not
especially popular among
rural women -- False to correct
The chapter states only that pills are very effective with lesser side effects and are well
accepted by the females and that Saheli is a once-a-week pill, but it never distinguishes rural
from urban users. In fact, IUDs are described as one of the most widely accepted methods
in India, so singling out pills as rural-popular is not supported.
